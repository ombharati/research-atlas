# From Fragmentation to Actionable Prototypes: Addressing Unresolved Technical Gaps in Rural Indian Digital Health

## Systemic Fragmentation as the Core Challenge in Rural Healthcare

The investigation into post-diagnosis care for rural Indian patients reveals that a significant portion of systemic failures is not attributable to the physical unavailability of medicines but to profound fragmentation in information flow [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules)]. India's healthcare system is characterized by extreme structural fragmentation, with over 1.69 million public health facilities operating alongside an estimated 80% of outpatient visits being handled by private providers [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. This dual-system reality inherently creates silos where patient records—encompassing clinical notes, laboratory results, imaging, prescriptions, and vital signs—are stored as independent information units at each facility where they were generated [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)]. This lack of continuity becomes a critical failure point when a patient seeks care from a different provider or moves between different levels of the healthcare hierarchy, such as from a Primary Health Centre (PHC) to a Community Health Centre (CHC) or a tertiary hospital [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. The consequence is a preventable event where a patient relocating from Chennai to Pune may require a duplicate CT scan because their prior medical history was not accessible to the new provider, illustrating how fragmented information directly undermines the principles of continuity of care [[12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)].

This fragmentation is a well-documented root cause of medication errors, which are defined as any preventable event leading to inappropriate medication use or patient harm [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules)]. These errors can occur at various stages of the medication journey, including prescribing, transcription, and dispensing, and are often rooted in poor communication and incomplete information [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules)]. A prospective study conducted at a government tertiary care hospital in eastern India starkly quantified this issue, identifying a total of 7,626 prescription errors across just 731 prescriptions [[2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)]. The study found that 77.72% of these errors were omissions, highlighting a pervasive issue with the completeness of prescription data [[2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)]. Critically, 72.09% of prescriptions lacked essential instructions or warnings for the patient, while the route of administration and duration of treatment were frequently omitted pieces of information [[2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)]. Furthermore, 11.9% of prescriptions were reported to be illegible, a problem that has historically plagued handwritten documents, with some reports indicating over half of all prescriptions are unreadable [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules), [2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)]. The use of unauthorized abbreviations was also observed in 28.18% of prescribed medications, further contributing to ambiguity and potential error [[2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)]. These findings demonstrate that even in formal healthcare settings, the foundational information required for safe medication use is often deficient before any digital system is introduced. Errors related to medication strength were found in nearly half of all prescribed drugs, representing another major safety risk [[2](https://www.ijbcp.com/index.php/ijbcp/article/view/719)].

Beyond prescriptions, the broader context of rural healthcare delivery is fraught with challenges that exacerbate information fragmentation. There is a documented shortage of medical personnel in rural areas, with a 7% shortfall at PHCs and a staggering 57% vacancy rate for specialist positions at CHCs [[7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12982-024-00271-1)]. This scarcity places immense pressure on available staff and contributes to diagnostic inefficiencies, increasing the reliance on managing chronic diseases [[14](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/15817)]. When community health workers (CHWs) or general practitioners (GPs) attempt to manage complex cases, the lack of access to a patient's complete medical history severely hinders their ability to provide safe and effective recommendations, especially during teleconsultations [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. The current eSanjeevani platform, despite its scale, has been criticized for consultants not having access to patients' past medical records, even though it is stated to integrate with MyHealthRecord, which prevents safe prescribing practices and continuity of care [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. Similarly, the platform's reliance on programmatic aggregate data without patient-level tracking via unique identifiers like the Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) means it cannot determine repeat visit patterns or assess continuity of care effectively [[16](https://academic.oup.com/oodh/article/doi/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025/8276784), [18](https://www.ictworks.org/esanjeevani-shows-what-actually-works/)]. This lack of individual-level data tracking obscures equity issues and makes it impossible to evaluate patient-level outcomes, a fundamental limitation for any system aiming to improve care pathways [[18](https://www.ictworks.org/esanjeevani-shows-what-actually-works/)].

The communication dynamics among the various stakeholders in the care process are also severely strained by this fragmentation. Patients and caregivers often struggle to understand prescriptions, dosage instructions, and the rationale behind treatments, a challenge compounded by language barriers and low digital literacy [[8](https://nextbillion.net/milestone-modernization-indian-healthcare-digital-integration-transforming-health-system/), [17](https://www.impriindia.com/insights/policy-update/esanjeevani-indias-national-telemedicine-initiative/)]. Pharmacists, who are crucial nodes in the medication supply chain, face difficulties in securely sharing patient information with other providers, with one study finding that 46% of pharmacists find it "somewhat or very difficult" to do so [[23](https://surescripts.com/insights/advancing-pharmacy-interoperability)]. This lack of coordination can lead to missed opportunities for interventions, such as providing peer support for medication adherence, which had very low uptake in one trial due to concerns about burdening family members or HIPAA compliance issues stemming from the inability to integrate texting platforms with Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [[27](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10865-023-00446-2)]. Referrals, a cornerstone of secondary and tertiary care, are particularly vulnerable to breakdowns. Traditional referral systems suffer from "Breakdowns Across Multiple Organizations," where different entities use distinct software, processes, and communication methods, resulting in fragmented care journeys [[29](https://www.socialroots.ai/blog/closed-loop/closed-loop-referral-challenges)]. Studies show that up to 38.9% of scheduling attempts have no appointment dates recorded, and referring providers often do not receive vital documentation and feedback from specialists outside their network, creating a cycle of lost referrals and duplicated efforts [[30](https://www.planstreet.com/closed-loop-referrals-guide)]. Even within the eSanjeevani telemedicine platform, the referral pathway is described as a single-step, isolated event with no mechanism for immediate re-referral to the correct specialist or for providing feedback-on-record to the referring health-worker, preventing continuous learning and audit cycles [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. This operational inefficiency leads to specialists being inundated with inappropriate referrals for simple complaints that should have been addressed by a GP, suboptimal resource utilization, and medically useless consultations consisting of only a few words of text like 'pain' or 'contraception' [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/), [17](https://www.impriindia.com/insights/policy-update/esanjeevani-indias-national-telemedicine-initiative/)]. These systemic failures, driven by the absence of a unified information infrastructure, create a fertile ground for adverse events and compromise the quality of care, particularly for vulnerable rural populations.

## The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM): A Framework for Integrated Data Exchange

In response to the deep-seated fragmentation plaguing India's healthcare system, the Government of India launched the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) as a national flagship initiative to build an integrated digital health ecosystem [[3](https://www.pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=2094604), [4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. The mission is predicated on the creation of shared infrastructure to enable the secure and seamless exchange of health data among various actors, including patients, healthcare providers, and insurers, thereby addressing the core problem of clinical data fragmentation [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [11](https://nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/)]. The architectural design of ABDM is built upon four interconnected layers, aiming to establish a robust foundation for interoperability across the nation [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare), [5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. At its base lies Layer 1, which leverages foundational public services such as Aadhaar for identity, UPI for payments, e-sign for authentication, and Digilocker for record storage [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. Building on this foundation, Layer 2 consists of domain-specific components, most notably the three core registries: the Health Facility Registry (HFR), the Healthcare Professionals Registry (HPR), and the Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. The HFR serves as a verified directory of enrolled healthcare facilities, while the HPR is a repository of individual practitioners, creating a trusted network of participants [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [26](https://www.dreamsoft4u.com/blog/abdm-api-integration-guide)]. The ABHA provides every citizen with a unique 14-digit identifier, intended to serve as the single thread connecting all their disparate health records from different providers [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [28](https://www.vcdoctor.com/blog/abdm-integration-checklist)].

The central mechanism for data exchange is Layer 3, known as the Unified Health Interface (UHI), an open network of Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) that facilitates real-time data exchange between applications [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare), [42](https://health.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/policy/nadda-launches-unified-health-interface-for-seamless-healthcare-access/132081750)]. This layer acts as a gateway, routing service requests through the ABDM Gateway developed by the National Health Authority (NHA) [[42](https://health.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/policy/nadda-launches-unified-health-interface-for-seamless-healthcare-access/132081750)]. Access to data is governed by Layer 4, which encompasses user-facing applications like the e-Sanjeevani telemedicine platform and provider-facing systems [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. A critical component of this architecture is the Health Information Exchange and Consent Manager (HIE-CM), which enforces a strict consent-based model [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)]. In this framework, entities that generate health records are designated as Health Information Providers (HIPs), while those requesting access are Health Information Users (HIUs) [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. Data exchange can only occur when a patient explicitly grants permission via a "consent artifact," ensuring that individuals retain control over their sensitive health information [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. This federated architecture, where patient data remains at the originating facility and is only shared upon request and consent, is designed to enhance privacy and avoid the risks associated with a single, centralized data repository [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)].

To ensure uniformity and interoperability, ABDM mandates the use of international standards for all clinical data exchanged within its ecosystem [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. The core transport standard is HL7 FHIR R4, augmented by a comprehensive FHIR Implementation Guide (IG) developed by the National Resource Centre for EHR Standards (NRCeS) in India [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [33](https://github.com/PSMRI/AMRIT-Docs/blob/main/architecture/integrations/abdm-fhir-developer-intro/README.md)]. This IG defines mandatory "Profiles" that specify the minimum conformance requirements for exchanging various types of clinical data, ensuring consistency across different systems [[11](https://nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/)]. For example, it specifies the use of ICD-10, SNOMED CT, and LOINC for coding clinical concepts, procedures, and laboratory observations, respectively [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [22](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kishoresnair_ai-activity-7415772272914911232-5NJT)]. This structured approach extends to specific clinical documents, with the IG defining standardized FHIR bundle types for scenarios like outpatient consultations (`OPConsultRecord`), prescriptions (`PrescriptionRecord`), and discharge summaries (`DischargeSummaryRecord`) [[11](https://nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/), [32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. The system also incorporates an approved Drug Registry, currently in the design phase, intended to provide a single source of truth for all medicines in India, further standardizing medication-related information [[8](https://nextbillion.net/milestone-modernization-indian-healthcare-digital-integration-transforming-health-system/), [21](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2278991)]. To support developers in building compliant systems, ABDM provides a sandbox environment for testing integration processes before products go live, although additional approvals may still be required post-testing [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare)]. Despite these ambitious components and a massive scale-up effort—with over 866 million ABHA accounts created by March 2026 and millions of digitized records linked—the journey towards functional interoperability is fraught with unresolved technical challenges that hinder its effectiveness in bridging information gaps for post-diagnosis care [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)].

## Critical Data Model and Semantic Deficiencies in ABDM

Despite the comprehensive architecture of the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM), a detailed analysis of its technical specifications reveals significant deficiencies in its data models and semantic frameworks. These gaps represent unresolved technical challenges that prevent the seamless flow of information necessary for safe and effective post-diagnosis care, such as accurate medication reconciliation and efficient referral management. The core issue lies in the mismatch between the complexity of clinical workflows and the granularity of the standardized data objects defined in the ABDM FHIR Implementation Guide. While the mandate for FHIR R4 and standardized terminologies like SNOMED CT and LOINC is a step toward interoperability, the specific implementation falls short in several critical areas, creating new points of failure and ambiguity [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [11](https://nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/)].

One of the most glaring gaps is the lack of end-to-end linkage in the medication journey. A patient's medication history is a dynamic entity that evolves from consultation to prescription to dispensing. However, the ABDM data models fail to capture this continuum cohesively. An outpatient consultation (`OPConsultRecord`) might document medications using a combination of `MedicationStatement` resources (for medications the patient is already taking) and `MedicationRequest` resources (for new prescriptions) [[32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. Later, a pharmacy that dispenses the medication would generate a separate `PrescriptionRecord` bundle. This bundle contains only `MedicationRequest` resources, corresponding to the drugs dispensed [[32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. Crucially, there is no standard mechanism or reference linking the set of `MedicationRequest` resources from the pharmacy's bundle back to the original consultation's records. This creates a fragmented view of the patient's true medication history; a clinician accessing the prescription record will see the new drugs but will have no easy way to connect them to the consultation that ordered them or to reconcile them with the patient's full list of current medications. This directly undermines the practice of medication reconciliation, a key strategy for reducing discrepancies and preventing harmful errors during transitions of care [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules)]. The fragmentation forces clinicians to manually piece together a patient's medication history from disparate sources, reintroducing the very human factors and communication breakdowns that ABDM aims to solve [[1](https://rxce.com/webmaterials/medication-errors-florida-reporting-rules)].

Another critical deficiency lies in the inadequate modeling of data required for operational pharmacy workflows. The FHIR `MedicationRequest` resource is designed to represent the clinical intent of a prescription order, making it suitable for inclusion in a patient's electronic medical record [[6](https://nirmitee.io/blog/pharmacy-eprescribing-ncpdp-script-fhir-medicationrequest-gap/)]. However, it lacks fields for critical transactional data essential for a "closed-loop" e-prescribing system. For instance, it does not contain standardized fields for Dispense As Written (DAW) codes (which indicate whether a brand-name drug should be substituted with a generic equivalent), fill status notifications (`RxFill` messages) that inform prescribers when a prescription has been filled, or prior authorization numbers required for certain specialty drugs [[6](https://nirmitee.io/blog/pharmacy-eprescribing-ncpdp-script-fhir-medicationrequest-gap/)]. The experience in the United States demonstrates that production e-prescribing ecosystems require a dual-layer architecture: an EHR generates a `MedicationRequest` for the clinical record, while simultaneously translating this information into a NCPDP SCRIPT message for transmission to a pharmacy network [[6](https://nirmitee.io/blog/pharmacy-eprescribing-ncpdp-script-fhir-medicationrequest-gap/)]. This necessitates a complex synchronization layer to keep both records updated with changes like substitutions or fill confirmations. ABDM's current framework does not formally recognize or standardize this transactional layer, leaving a significant gap for a software prototype that could act as this bridge, enriching the FHIR model with operational metadata or providing a standardized format for pharmacy transactions.

Furthermore, ABDM's handling of unstructured data represents a missed opportunity for automation and intelligence. The framework relies heavily on the `HealthDocumentRecord` bundle type, which is designed to wrap unstructured documents like scanned referral letters, doctor's notes, or lab reports [[32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. This is achieved by encoding the entire document content as a base64 string within a `Binary` resource, referenced by a `DocumentReference` [[32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. While this preserves the integrity of the document, it treats valuable clinical information as opaque text. This approach fundamentally prevents machine-readable extraction of key data points such as the name of the referring doctor, the specific reason for referral, the date of the letter, or required follow-up actions. Instead of enabling automated processing, this method forces clinicians to manually review these documents, negating the benefits of digitization. A more advanced system would leverage Natural Language Processing (NLP) to parse these documents and extract structured data into appropriate FHIR resources (e.g., populating the `referralSource` field in a `ServiceRequest`), but ABDM's current specification does not provide a standardized way to handle this transformation.

Finally, persistent challenges in terminology mapping and data semantics undermine the goal of a truly unified health record. ABDM mandates the use of SNOMED CT for recording data at the point of care and ICD-10 for reporting purposes, with data in SNOMED CT being mapped to ICD codes for official statistics [[22](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kishoresnair_ai-activity-7415772272914911232-5NJT)]. While this is a sound principle, the practical process of converting proprietary data formats and local codes into these standard terminologies is a known bottleneck in healthcare interoperability [[40](https://edenlab.io/blog/ai-data-mapping-in-healthcare)]. It is time-consuming, requires specialized expertise, and is often costly, leading many organizations to leave data siloed [[40](https://edenlab.io/blog/ai-data-mapping-in-healthcare)]. Without a robust, standardized mechanism for handling these mappings, inconsistencies can persist. For example, a condition might be recorded using different terms or codes in different systems, which could be treated as distinct events by an AI model unless a sophisticated semantic layer normalizes them [[39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)]. The current framework does not adequately address how to resolve these semantic variations, which can corrupt the outputs of any downstream analytical or AI-driven application. This highlights a critical need for tools that can provide AI-ready interoperability by resolving patient identity and normalizing clinical terminology across disparate sources [[39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)].

## Infrastructure and Operational Barriers to Seamless Care Delivery

While ABDM provides a blueprint for data exchange, its practical implementation in rural India is severely constrained by critical infrastructure and operational deficiencies. The promise of a connected digital health ecosystem is undermined by the harsh realities of low-connectivity environments and the inherent limitations of current software architectures. A significant barrier is the widespread lack of consistent internet connectivity, particularly in rural regions, which makes the full functionality of a consent-based, online record-sharing model less accessible than in urban areas [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)]. This digital divide is a fundamental challenge that must be addressed for any digital health solution to succeed at the last mile. The BBNL project aims to provide high-speed optical fiber to gram panchayats, but progress is ongoing and patchy [[7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12982-024-00271-1)]. Until reliable connectivity is ubiquitous, any software prototype must be designed with resilience to intermittent or non-existent network conditions.

A core architectural paradigm that is largely absent from current rural-focused digital health tools is the "offline-first" design [[31](https://nextbillion.net/designing-digital-health-tools-for-last-mile-lessons-for-maximizing-connectivity-usability-trust/)]. Most applications are built on a "cloud-first" model, assuming constant connectivity, which renders them unusable in low-connectivity settings. In contrast, an offline-first architecture treats the device itself as the primary source of truth, allowing users to capture and interact with data locally without any network dependency [[35](https://developer.android.com/topic/architecture/data-layer/offline-first), [36](https://www.alphasoftware.com/blog/offline-sync-architecture-tutorial-examples-tools-for-field-operations)]. This design philosophy is essential for frontline health workers and patients in rural India. Successful implementations in similar contexts, such as the CommCare-based workflows in Jharkhand and Bihar, demonstrate the viability of this approach [[31](https://nextbillion.net/designing-digital-health-tools-for-last-mile-lessons-for-maximizing-connectivity-usability-trust/)]. These systems allow data to be stored on-device and automatically sync to a central server whenever a network signal becomes available, preventing data loss and workflow interruption [[31](https://nextbillion.net/designing-digital-health-tools-for-last-mile-lessons-for-maximizing-connectivity-usability-trust/)]. An effective offline-first system employs several key principles: treating the local device storage as the canonical data source, queuing all operations locally, and synchronizing only the changes ("deltas") since the last successful sync to minimize bandwidth consumption [[36](https://www.alphasoftware.com/blog/offline-sync-architecture-tutorial-examples-tools-for-field-operations), [38](https://www.buntulabs.com/blog/offline-first-architecture)]. Key technologies like Android's WorkManager can be used to implement persistent queues and automatic retry logic for synchronization tasks [[35](https://developer.android.com/topic/architecture/data-layer/offline-first)]. There is a clear and pressing need for software prototypes that embed this offline-first logic into ABDM-compliant applications, ensuring that critical functions like capturing patient data, issuing referrals, and managing medication lists remain functional regardless of network status.

Compounding the connectivity issue are significant operational inefficiencies, particularly within the eSanjeevani telemedicine platform, which is a central component of ABDM [[16](https://academic.oup.com/oodh/article/doi/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025/8276784)]. The current referral pathway is described as a single-step, isolated event with no feedback loop, a major failure point in care coordination [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. Once a specialist consultant completes a teleconsultation, there is no straightforward mechanism for them to provide documented feedback to the referring health worker or to immediately re-referral the patient to another specialist if the initial referral was inappropriate [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. This breaks the cycle of care and prevents institutional learning. A closed-loop referral system, which tracks a referral from its creation through scheduling, service delivery, outcome reporting, and final closure, is a recognized solution to these problems [[29](https://www.socialroots.ai/blog/closed-loop/closed-loop-referral-challenges)]. Such a system would require standardized statuses (e.g., Draft → Sent → Received → Accepted → Scheduled → Completed) and automated escalations to keep the process moving [[29](https://www.socialroots.ai/blog/closed-loop/closed-loop-referral-challenges)]. The technical implementation would involve creating a centralized view of all referrals and integrating the workflow with the EHRs of all involved parties, a level of interoperability that is not currently present in eSanjeevani [[30](https://www.planstreet.com/closed-loop-referrals-guide)]. A software prototype could address this by creating a dedicated module that transforms the linear, disconnected referral process into a dynamic, multi-step workflow with clear ownership and visibility for all stakeholders.

Furthermore, the lack of individual-level patient tracking within the eSanjeevani platform is a fundamental operational flaw that limits its utility for assessing continuity of care. Research revealed that as of September 2023, the platform was unable to accurately track beneficiaries using unique identifiers like the ABHA, forcing it to rely on programmatic aggregate data [[16](https://academic.oup.com/oodh/article/doi/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025/8276784)]. This means it cannot distinguish between new and repeat consultations or determine unique users, obscuring patterns of care and hindering the evaluation of patient outcomes [[16](https://academic.oup.com/oodh/article/doi/10.1093/oodh/oqaf025/8276784), [18](https://www.ictworks.org/esanjeevani-shows-what-actually-works/)]. This is a critical gap for managing chronic diseases, which require longitudinal monitoring. While the platform has evolved, with version 2.0 introducing features like prescription synchronization, the foundational capability for individual-level tracking remains a challenge [[18](https://www.ictworks.org/esanjeevani-shows-what-actually-works/)]. A prototype focused on enhancing the platform's backend to reliably associate every consultation with an ABHA ID would provide a longitudinal, portable health record, a key feature for improving continuity of care [[17](https://www.impriindia.com/insights/policy-update/esanjeevani-indias-national-telemedicine-initiative/)]. This would allow a patient's complete history of teleconsultations to be accessed by any authorized provider, facilitating safer prescribing and better management of follow-up appointments and chronic conditions. Without this basic tracking capability, eSanjeevani operates as a series of disconnected encounters rather than a cohesive care pathway, limiting its impact on patient health outcomes.

## Persistent Identity Resolution and Matching Challenges

Even with the introduction of a unique national identifier like the Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA), achieving a single, unified patient identity across India's heterogeneous healthcare landscape remains a formidable technical challenge. The existence of a unique ID is a necessary but insufficient condition for solving data fragmentation. The core problem is one of identity resolution: the inability to reliably link disparate records belonging to the same individual when they are captured in different systems, by different providers, or under slightly different demographic details. This challenge is magnified in a system where 80% of outpatient visits are handled by private providers whose systems may not be perfectly synchronized with the public sector [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. While ABDM's federated architecture relies on the ABHA to create a single source of truth for identity establishment, its success depends on the correct creation and consistent linking of this ID to a patient's records across all participating facilities [[4](https://medblocks.com/blog/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-ushering-in-the-upi-moment-of-healthcare), [26](https://www.dreamsoft4u.com/blog/abdm-api-integration-guide)].

The primary technical hurdle arises when a deterministic match based on a single shared identifier is not possible. This can happen due to administrative errors, such as mismatches between an ABHA created with Aadhaar details and the patient's actual demographic information, leading to duplicate IDs [[28](https://www.vcdoctor.com/blog/abdm-integration-checklist)]. In many real-world scenarios, especially when dealing with legacy data or records from non-integrated systems, a common unique identifier may not exist. In these cases, a Master Patient Index (MPI) is required to perform probabilistic matching [[19](https://nirmitee.io/blog/fhir-data-synchronization-between-hospitals/)]. An MPI uses algorithms to compare demographic data—such as name, date of birth, gender, and address—to identify potential matches between records [[19](https://nirmitee.io/blog/fhir-data-synchronization-between-hospitals/)]. The decision of which patient records to share and how to match them across different hospital systems is identified as the decision with the highest clinical risk; a failure here can lead to wrong-patient matches, which are clinical safety incidents [[19](https://nirmitee.io/blog/fhir-data-synchronization-between-hospitals/)]. While some sources mention Enterprise Master Patient Indexes (EMPIs) as a scalable solution to overcome the limitations of traditional patient matching, particularly using AI and machine learning, there is no evidence in the provided materials that ABDM has a centrally managed, robust MPI to resolve fragmented identities across the entire ecosystem [[13](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/data-fragmentation-key-healthcare-challenge-our-time)]. This gap necessitates the development of a prototype focused on AI-powered patient matching to intelligently connect records from different clinics, labs, pharmacies, and telemedicine platforms.

AI offers a powerful approach to this problem by training algorithms on historical human decisions to learn and replicate the complex rules of patient matching at a massive scale [[13](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/data-fragmentation-key-healthcare-challenge-our-time)]. Machine learning models can be trained to weigh different demographic fields and handle variations in spelling, formatting, and missing data, significantly improving accuracy compared to rule-based systems [[13](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/data-fragmentation-key-healthcare-challenge-our-time)]. A prototype for an AI-powered EMPI could operate within the ABDM framework, respecting its consent architecture. It could start by using the ABHA as a known identifier and then query different Health Information Provider (HIP) systems for records matching that ID. For records without an ABHA, the system could then employ a probabilistic matching engine to find potential duplicates across multiple systems. By providing a consolidated, de-duplicated view of a patient's record from their perspective, such a tool would empower them to share a complete and accurate history with any provider, directly addressing a major source of information fragmentation [[39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)]. The implementation of such a system requires careful consideration of conflict resolution strategies, such as 'last write wins' or more sophisticated Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), and robust security measures to protect data both at rest on the device and in transit [[36](https://www.alphasoftware.com/blog/offline-sync-architecture-tutorial-examples-tools-for-field-operations), [37](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393910615_Offline-First_Mobile_Architecture_Enhancing_Usability_and_Resilience_in_Mobile_Systems)].

The completeness and reliability of the underlying registries also pose a challenge. The Health Facility Registry (HFR) and Healthcare Professionals Registry (HPR) are essential for creating a trusted network, but their uptake varies significantly across states and between the public and private sectors [[8](https://nextbillion.net/milestone-modernization-indian-healthcare-digital-integration-transforming-health-system/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)]. Governance-level fragmentation, where each state operates its own health information systems, complicates data integration and interoperability [[8](https://nextbillion.net/milestone-modernization-indian-healthcare-digital-integration-transforming-health-system/)]. As of July 2025, while 4.17 lakh facilities were registered on the HFR, only approximately 3.2 lakh had ABDM-enabled software, and active usage was cited at just 256,542 facilities, highlighting a substantial gap between registration capacity and verified active integration [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/), [12](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India's-Health-Data-Problem/)]. This incomplete network affects the practical reach of ABDM's consent-based exchange layer. While not a direct software gap, a prototype could help mitigate this by providing tools for verifying and enriching registry data, or by developing more resilient connection protocols that can function even when some parts of the network are not fully integrated. Ultimately, the challenge of identity resolution is not merely a technical one but also involves governance, incentives for participation, and workflow integration, all of which are necessary for a federated system like ABDM to achieve its vision of a unified health record [[8](https://nextbillion.net/milestone-modernization-indian-healthcare-digital-integration-transforming-health-system/), [39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)].

## Identified Opportunities for an AI-Enhanced Software Prototype

The comprehensive analysis of systemic fragmentation, the capabilities and limitations of the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM), and the operational realities of rural healthcare in India reveals several concrete and actionable opportunities for an AI-enhanced software prototype. These opportunities are not aimed at replacing the existing national infrastructure but at augmenting it by addressing the specific, unresolved technical gaps that impede seamless post-diagnosis care. The proposed prototypes target deficiencies in data modeling, operational resilience, and identity resolution, offering pragmatic solutions that can function within the ABDM framework to bridge critical information gaps. These interventions are designed to improve medication safety, streamline care coordination, and empower patients and providers with a more complete and usable view of health information.

The first and most promising opportunity is the development of an **AI-Powered Semantic Enrichment Layer**. This prototype would function as a middleware application, sitting between disparate digital health systems (e.g., a clinic's EMR, a telemedicine platform, and a pharmacy's dispensing system). Its primary purpose would be to address the semantic and data model deficiencies identified in ABDM's current implementation. Firstly, it would utilize Natural Language Processing (NLP) to extract structured, machine-readable data from unstructured documents passed between providers, such as referral letters wrapped in ABDM's `HealthDocumentRecord` bundle [[32](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-fhir-bundles-patient-journey-hospital-visit-health-record/)]. Instead of treating these documents as opaque text, the prototype would parse them to populate standardized fields, automating the extraction of the referring doctor's name, reason for referral, and required follow-up actions. Secondly, it would act as an intelligent translator and normalizer. It would map disparate drug names and clinical terms used in local systems to the standardized codes in the ABDM Drug Registry and the SNOMED CT terminology, ensuring that concepts like 'MI', 'myocardial infarction', and 'heart attack' are consistently interpreted by AI models and clinical systems [[21](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2278991), [39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)]. Thirdly, it would address the critical gap in prescription data by augmenting the standard FHIR `MedicationRequest` resource with additional transactional metadata derived from pharmacy operations, such as Dispense As Written (DAW) codes and fill status notifications [[6](https://nirmitee.io/blog/pharmacy-eprescribing-ncpdp-script-fhir-medicationrequest-gap/)]. By enriching the data stream flowing between systems, this prototype would create richer, more actionable data objects that enable more sophisticated clinical decision support and automate workflows that are currently manual and error-prone.

The second major opportunity lies in creating an **Offline-First Closed-Loop Care Coordinator**. This prototype would be a mobile application designed specifically for the challenging connectivity environments of rural India. It would embody the "offline-first" architecture, where the device is the primary source of truth, allowing community health workers (CHWs) and patients to capture and manage data without an internet connection [[31](https://nextbillion.net/designing-digital-health-tools-for-last-mile-lessons-for-maximizing-connectivity-usability-trust/), [35](https://developer.android.com/topic/architecture/data-layer/offline-first)]. The app would enable CHWs to document patient encounters, create referrals, and track vital signs on-device, with data syncing automatically in the background whenever a network becomes available [[31](https://nextbillion.net/designing-digital-health-tools-for-last-mile-lessons-for-maximizing-connectivity-usability-trust/), [38](https://www.buntulabs.com/blog/offline-first-architecture)]. This directly addresses the operational failure of disconnected workflows seen in platforms like eSanjeevani [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422547/)]. A core feature of this prototype would be the implementation of a true closed-loop referral tracker. When a CHW initiates a referral, the system would provide a dashboard showing the referral's status (e.g., sent, received, accepted, scheduled, completed, or rejected), with updates pushed to the CHW's app by the receiving specialist. This would include a mandatory feedback mechanism, allowing the specialist to send notes and recommendations back to the referring CHW, closing the loop and enabling learning [[29](https://www.socialroots.ai/blog/closed-loop/closed-loop-referral-challenges)]. Additionally, the application could integrate medication reminders and adherence tracking, synchronizing with pharmacy refill data when available to alert the patient and CHW about potential gaps in supply, thus tackling medication non-adherence at the grassroots level.

The third opportunity is the development of a **Federated Patient Identity Resolver**. This prototype would be an AI-driven Enterprise Master Patient Index (EMPI) designed to operate within the constraints of ABDM's federated architecture. Its main function would be to solve the persistent problem of creating a single, accurate patient identity from fragmented records across multiple, non-interoperable systems. It would use machine learning and probabilistic matching algorithms to identify and merge duplicate patient records from different EMRs, labs, and pharmacies, even when they have minor variations in demographic data like name spellings or addresses [[13](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/data-fragmentation-key-healthcare-challenge-our-time), [19](https://nirmitee.io/blog/fhir-data-synchronization-between-hospitals/)]. The resolver would be designed to respect patient consent; it would start by using the ABHA as a known identifier and then query different Health Information Provider (HIP) systems for records matching that ID. For records without an ABHA, the system would use its matching engine to find potential duplicates across multiple systems [[5](https://caladriushealth.ai/blog/2026/06/05/India%27s-Health-Data-Problem/)]. The output of this prototype would be a consolidated, de-duplicated view of a patient's record, which the patient could access and choose to share with any provider. This would empower patients to overcome the "single source of truth" problem, ensuring that their complete medical history follows them and is available to any clinician who needs it, thereby reducing the risk of errors from incomplete information and supporting continuity of care [[39](https://rhapsody.health/use-cases/ai-ready-interoperability-healthcare-integration-infrastructure-for-ai/)]. Together, these three prototypes represent a strategic approach to leveraging technology to solve the specific, unresolved technical problems that currently compromise patient safety and care quality in rural India.
